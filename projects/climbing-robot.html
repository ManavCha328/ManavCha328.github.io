Suction climbing robot for GelBelt tactile inspection

A robot that carries the GelBelt tactile sensor up walls and across inverted and curved surfaces, so aircraft skins can be inspected by touch at high resolution.

Novel Mobile Robots Lab ↗RoboTouch Lab ↗

Challenge

Carry a tactile sensor across vertical, inverted, and curved surfaces while pressing it into the surface with at least 10 N.

Approach

Active suction cups on a roller-chain drive, a magnet-switched valve on every cup, and a spring suspension sized from measured cup stiffness.

Where it stands

Adhesion and suspension passed inverted and curved-surface tests. The full robot stays attached from flat to vertical and climbs a vertical panel.

01

Motivation

Aircraft skins and other large metal or composite structures need regular inspection for defects that can limit performance or safety. Doing it by hand is slow and error-prone.

Our answer is to put a high-resolution tactile sensor on a robot that can drive across the whole structure and collect the data with minimal human effort.

02

Design objectives

  • Force and weight: press the GelBelt into the surface with at least 10 N, while carrying about 25 N of sensor, drive, and vacuum hardware
  • Surface interaction: keep continuous, even contact for consistent mapping, and cover the full surface in every orientation
  • Robust and efficient: tolerate the rivets, seams, paint variation, and dust found on an aircraft wing
03

Adhesion selection

We tested suction cups on a force-torque rig and chose vinyl cups with active suction. One cup holds more than 9 N in shear, so three can carry the robot, and four touch the surface at once for redundancy. Active suction doubled shear holding over passive. Propellers were ruled out: one motor gave only about 5.2 N of thrust.

04

Suspension and validation

Springs sized from measured cup stiffness (14.4 N/cm) keep the GelBelt pressed in with more than 10 N. The module held on upside down, on a wall, and on a curved aluminum profile, with clean GelBelt images.

05

Central chassis

With adhesion proven, we built the full robot around a two-level chassis. Spring struts hold the top plate above the drive plate, so the GelBelt keeps its contact force as the cups below compress. The top plate carries the pump, servo controller, and computer, and routes the sensor cable and vacuum tubing.

06

Pneumatic system

One pump feeds every cup as they travel around the chain, so each cup needs its own valve: open when the cup is on the surface, closed everywhere else. The valve has to handle a rotary joint, seal under vacuum, and let the cup release cleanly.

Each valve is switched by an external magnet along the drive plate. The first version used a steel ball that leaked and stuck. The current one uses an internal magnet and a silicone O-ring, mounts directly to the chain, and held for one minute under a 2 kg load.

07

Drive

The cups ride on a roller chain, because a timing belt couldn’t take the twisting load from the robot’s weight. Lifting 2 kg at 4 cm/s needs about 0.32 N·m, well under the Dynamixel’s 0.93 N·m stall torque.

Testing the full robot drove three changes:

  • Two motors overloaded, so the drive moved to four Dynamixels
  • Sprockets on screw axes wobbled, so each sprocket and shaft became one part on two bearings
  • The drive plate flexed, so we added supporting beams
08

Electronics

A Raspberry Pi runs the robot and streams GelBelt images and motor data over Wi-Fi. It drives four XL-330 motors through a U2D2, and the vacuum pump sits on a mechanical switch.

09

Guide rails

On a vertical wall the chain sagged. Valves on slack sections tilted and jammed, and incoming cups couldn’t reach the surface or folded their lips when they did.

A guide rail now holds every valve on a fixed path, so the cups meet the surface in one even plane even though the chain isn’t rigid. Its profile gives each incoming cup the same speed along the surface as the cups already attached, and plastic bearings keep friction low.

10

MiniGelBelt calibration

We are calibrating MiniGelBelt, the sensor the robot carries, so its color images can be turned into surface geometry.

11

Where it stands

  • Adhesion and suspension are validated on inverted, wall, and curved surfaces
  • The assembled robot stays attached as its panel tilts from flat to vertical, and climbs the vertical panel
  • Next: test full-build contact behavior with suction and magnet valves together
  • Then measure: cup attachment success, straight-line stability, GelBelt contact consistency, reconstruction accuracy, motor current by orientation, turning radius, and weight margin
  • After that: curves and steering, and a paper
